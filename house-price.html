Case study 01 · Regression

House Price Prediction in Ames, Iowa

A CatBoost model that estimates what a house in Ames, Iowa sold for, from 77 details about the property. On 292 houses it never saw during training, its predictions were off by $15,022 on average.

01 · Dataset and exploration

Understanding the data

The dataset records 1,460 house sales in Ames, Iowa, between 2006 and 2010. Each sale has 81 columns: an ID, the sale price and 79 details about the property, from overall quality and living area to the garage, basement and neighborhood.

Before exploring, 20% of the houses (292) were set aside as a test set. The price and feature exploration, and every cleaning decision, used only the remaining 1,168 training houses, so the final score is measured on houses the process never touched.

02 · Cleaning and preprocessing

Preparing the data

Each cleaning decision was tested before it was kept: the same Linear Regression baseline was scored with 5-fold cross-validation, changing one decision at a time.

Two unfinished sales were removed from training. Houses 523 and 1298 have more than 4,000 sq ft of living area but sold for $184,750 and $160,000, because they were partial sales of houses not yet completed. They were removed from the training set only; the test set was left as it was.

The pool area column was dropped. Only 6 of the 1,168 training houses have a pool, too few to learn a reliable effect. The pool quality column still tells the model whether a house has one. The ID and the garage build year were also left out.

Six models were compared on the 1,166 cleaned training houses, with the same preprocessing, the same 5-fold split and the settings chosen during tuning.

CatBoost, LightGBM and XGBoost finished effectively tied: their average errors are within $343 of each other, less than their fold-to-fold spread. CatBoost had the lowest average error, and a grid search did not beat its default settings.

A stack of the three boosters was $93 better, a fifth of its own spread, so the simpler single model was kept. The model predicts the price in dollars directly; the log transform suggested during exploration was not used.

04 · Model evaluation

Measured on unseen houses

The 292 test houses were scored once, after every choice had been made. Both models were trained on the same 1,166 houses with the same preprocessing.

CatBoost’s average error is about 16% lower than the Linear Regression baseline’s, the same gap seen in cross-validation. The test error is $584 higher than the cross-validation error, within the fold-to-fold spread: the settings were chosen on those folds, so the cross-validation scores were slightly optimistic.

05 · Limitations and key takeaways

What the model can and can’t say

Limitations

  • One city and five years of sales: the model has not been tested on houses outside Ames or after 2010.
  • The test set has 292 houses and was scored once, so the result carries real uncertainty; no confidence interval was computed.
  • Importance shows what the model relies on, not what causes a higher price. With only 292 test houses, values under about $1,000 may be noise and should not be ranked against each other.
  • The two large unfinished sales were removed from training, so the model has not learned from houses like them.
  • A local Streamlit app lets the price be explored for 10 of the 77 inputs; it is not published online.

Key takeaways

Three things this project showed

  1. Testing cleaning decisions pays off. Two small changes lifted the baseline’s R² from 0.756 to 0.890 and fixed its worst fold.
  2. A strong baseline keeps results honest. Linear Regression was hard to beat, and CatBoost’s 16% gain is measured against it.
  3. Simpler won a tie. When the top models were within their own noise, the single default CatBoost model was kept over a stack.
